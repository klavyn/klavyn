Chorded typing, from scratch

The whole idea in a few minutes — then the two techniques that make it work on a normal keyboard.

the core idea

k l o → look

One press of a word's letters, one word out. That's the entire concept — the rest is technique and practice.

# What is chorded typing?

Normal typing is sequential: one key, one letter, in order. Chorded typing is simultaneous: you press a small set of keys as a group — a "chord," like on a piano — and get a whole word. Court stenographers hit ~300 words per minute this way. Dedicated hardware like the CharaChorder is built for it.

klavyn brings the core skill to the keyboard you already have. You press the letters of a word — in any order — and it swaps what you typed for the word.

# Order doesn't matter

A chord is an unordered set of letters. To type look you press l, o, k (the repeated o collapses) — and it doesn't matter whether your fingers land as k-l-o, o-l-k, or all at once. Less to memorize: you already know a word's letters.

Because chords are sets, a few words collide — {a,e,r} could be are, ear, or era. klavyn keeps the most common word per set so every chord resolves to exactly one word.

# Hold vs. roll

There are two ways to enter a chord, and you'll mix both:

⬇

Hold

Press the letters together and release. Fast and natural for short, 2–4 letter chords your hand can span at once.

〜

Roll

Press and release letters in a quick ripple, each within the roll-gap window. This is how long words work — you never hold more than a couple of keys at any instant.

The roll gap defaults to 200 ms (tune it with --roll-gap-ms). As long as each new press starts before the keyboard "goes idle" for that long, klavyn treats the ripple as one chord.

# Why rolling matters: rollover

Most non-gaming keyboards can only report a handful of simultaneous keys — commonly six. That ceiling is called key rollover. If chording meant holding every letter at once, a six-key ceiling would cap you at six-letter words.

Rolling sidesteps the ceiling entirely: at any single instant you're only holding one or two keys, so a seven-, eight-, nine-letter word is no problem. Measure your own ceiling with:

$ klavyn detect-rollover

It reports the most simultaneous keys your OS and keyboard actually deliver — the real number, end-to-end, not a spec sheet. Anything longer than that, you'll roll.

# Building the skill

  1. Start in --dry-run. Watch klavyn recognize chords without touching your text, so mistakes cost nothing.
  2. Pick ten common words. the, and, you, that, have… drill those chords until they're automatic before adding more.
  3. Hold the short ones, roll the long ones. Let your hands learn which words feel like a grab and which feel like a ripple.
  4. Tune the roll gap. Getting accidental corrections during fast normal typing? Lower --roll-gap-ms. Chords not registering? Raise it.
  5. Turn off dry-run once the muscle memory is there, and let klavyn do the corrections for real.

If you later buy a CharaChorder, the skill transfers directly — same idea, dedicated hardware.
